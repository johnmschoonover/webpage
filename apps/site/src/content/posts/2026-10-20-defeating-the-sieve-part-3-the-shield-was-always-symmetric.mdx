In [Part 1](/writing/2025-11-30-defeating-the-sieve) I claimed that twin primes were "exposed" to the number 3: if <Latex formula="p \equiv 1 \pmod 3" />, then <Latex formula="2p + 1" /> is divisible by 3, so half of all Gap 2 sums get shot down. That was the example the whole series was built on.

It is false. If <Latex formula="p \equiv 1 \pmod 3" />, then <Latex formula="p + 2 \equiv 0 \pmod 3" />, so <Latex formula="p + 2" /> is not prime and <Latex formula="(p, p+2)" /> is not a twin pair. Every twin prime above 3 sits at <Latex formula="p \equiv 2 \pmod 3" />, which puts the sum at <Latex formula="2p + 1 \equiv 2 \pmod 3" />. Gap 2 is never divisible by 3. It is shielded.

[Part 2](/writing/2025-12-07-defeating-the-sieve-part-2-the-mod-3-trap) actually made this exact argument for Gap 14 and called it the Neighbor Shield. I did not notice that it applied to Gap 2 as well, or that it contradicted Part 1. This post is the correction, and the correction turns out to be more interesting than the original claim.

## The rule I should have written down

Fix an odd prime <Latex formula="q" /> and a gap <Latex formula="g" />. Both parents <Latex formula="p" /> and <Latex formula="p + g" /> are prime, so <Latex formula="p" /> avoids two residues modulo <Latex formula="q" />: it is not <Latex formula="0" />, and it is not <Latex formula="-g" />. The sum <Latex formula="S = 2p + g - 1" /> is divisible by <Latex formula="q" /> for exactly one residue of <Latex formula="p" />, namely <Latex formula="p \equiv (1 - g)/2" />. So the question is whether that one bad residue is among the two the parents already rule out.

- If <Latex formula="(1-g)/2 \equiv 0" />, that is <Latex formula="g \equiv 1" />, the bad residue is already excluded.
- If <Latex formula="(1-g)/2 \equiv -g" />, that is <Latex formula="g \equiv -1" />, the bad residue is already excluded.

Both signs work. The shield condition is <Latex formula="g \equiv \pm 1 \pmod q" />, for every odd prime including 3. My original rule used <Latex formula="+1" /> for 3 and <Latex formula="-1" /> for everything else, which is half of the truth at every prime.

There are two more cases, and they explain the low end of the spectrum, which Part 1 ignored entirely. Measured against a random odd integer, which survives <Latex formula="q" /> with probability <Latex formula="(q-1)/q" />:

| Case | Condition | Chance <Latex formula="S" /> is hit by <Latex formula="q" /> | Factor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shield | <Latex formula="g \equiv \pm 1 \pmod q" /> | 0 | <Latex formula="\frac{q}{q-1}" /> |
| Trap | <Latex formula="g \equiv 0 \pmod q" /> | 1 in <Latex formula="q-1" /> | <Latex formula="\frac{q(q-2)}{(q-1)^2}" /> |
| Neutral | otherwise | 1 in <Latex formula="q-2" /> | <Latex formula="\frac{q(q-3)}{(q-1)(q-2)}" /> |

The trap row is Part 2's Mod 3 Trap, and at <Latex formula="q = 3" /> the factor is <Latex formula="3 \cdot 1 / 4 = 0.75" />, the number I found by hand. The neutral row is new. It is a mild penalty, because the parents exclude two residues from the pool, which makes the one bad residue slightly more likely than it would be for an unconstrained number.

The prediction for a gap is the product of these factors over every odd prime, times the chance a random odd number of the same size is prime, which is <Latex formula="2 / \ln S" />.

## Gap 4 beats Gap 2 by exactly three halves

Here is the cleanest test the rule offers. Gaps 2 and 4 are both shielded at 3. For every prime from 7 upward, both are neutral, since neither is 0 or <Latex formula="\pm 1" /> modulo anything that large. The only prime that treats them differently is 5: Gap 4 is <Latex formula="-1 \pmod 5" /> and shielded, Gap 2 is neutral.

<Latex formula="\frac{\text{Gap 4}}{\text{Gap 2}} = \frac{5/4}{5 \cdot 2 / (4 \cdot 3)} = \frac{3}{2}" block />

Observed at <Latex formula="10^{10}" />: <Latex formula="0.1439 / 0.0959 = 1.500" />.

The model runs about half a percentage point low on Gap 12 and nearly a full point low on Gap 94, the largest miss among gaps with at least 100,000 pairs. That is the expected signature of Hardy–Littlewood secondary terms, which decay slowly with <Latex formula="N" />, and it is the same kind of residual Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan saw in their data. The ordering of every gap in the table is predicted correctly, including Gap 210 landing below Gap 2 and Gap 30 landing below everything.

The old chart in the report plotted a "boost" with no units against the observed rate and drew a best-fit line through it. The new chart plots the predicted rate against the observed rate with the line <Latex formula="y = x" />. Points that sit on that line are not fitted. They are predicted.

## Gap 34, properly counted

Gap 34 was the king of Part 1 as a triple shield against 3, 5 and 7. Under the symmetric rule it is also <Latex formula="1 \pmod{11}" />, so it is a quadruple shield. The clean way to say it: <Latex formula="33 \cdot 35 = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11" />. A gap is shielded by every odd prime up to <Latex formula="p_k" /> exactly when <Latex formula="g^2 - 1" /> is divisible by <Latex formula="3 \cdot 5 \cdots p_k" />. It is also <Latex formula="2 \times 17" />, so 17 is a trap for it, worth a penalty of 0.4%.

The sequence of smallest fully shielded gaps under the symmetric rule is 2, 4, 34, 34, 274, 9164, 83656, 937364. Under the one-sided rule the repository's Python tool generates it is 4, 4, 34, 1924, 25024, 85084. Neither is in the OEIS as of this writing, which I checked against the two sequences that are: the primes <Latex formula="p" /> for which <Latex formula="p + \text{nextprime}(p) - 1" /> is prime, and the primes that result. Those were already there.

## This is a conjecture from 1904

I should have gone looking for prior work earlier. The question "for which gaps can a small prime block all three of <Latex formula="n" />, <Latex formula="n+g" /> and <Latex formula="2n+g-1" /> at once" is the admissibility condition in Dickson's conjecture, stated in 1904 for exactly this kind of system of linear forms. Hardy and Littlewood put the quantitative factors on it in 1923. Bateman and Horn generalized it in 1962. The table above is the Bateman–Horn local factor for this system, conditioned on the first two forms being prime. The <Latex formula="2/\ln S" /> baseline is the prime number theorem.

The closest modern relative is Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan's 2016 paper on biases in consecutive primes, which found that the residues of neighboring primes are correlated in surprising ways and explained it with the same Hardy–Littlewood machinery. The shape of this series, an empirical bias in consecutive primes explained by residue counting, is that paper's shape.

So the mechanism is old. What I could not find anywhere is this particular statistic. Nobody seems to have tallied the primality of <Latex formula="p_n + p_{n+1} - 1" /> by gap, and the <Latex formula="10^{10}" /> census appears to be the first measurement of it.

## Can you use it to find primes?

This was the question I most wanted to answer, so here is the honest version. If you have two consecutive primes at a favorable gap, their sum-minus-one really is two to three times more likely to be prime than a random odd number of its size. That is a real prior, and it is tempting to use it for triage: spend the expensive primality test on the candidate with the best parent gap.

The catch is that the gap carries no information about <Latex formula="S" /> beyond its residues modulo small primes, and once you have <Latex formula="p" /> and <Latex formula="g" /> you have <Latex formula="S" /> itself and can read those residues off directly. Trial division is the same test, carried out exactly instead of statistically, and it does not stop at 11.

| Screen applied to the candidate | Prior boost over a random odd number |
|---|---|
| Quadruple shield (Gap 34) | about 2.3x |
| No odd prime factor below 100 | about 4x |
| No odd prime factor below <Latex formula="10^6" /> | about 12x |

The last row is what every large prime search already does before spending a real test. The shield is a slower route to a weaker version of it. What survives is not a search technique. It is a picture: the sum inherits a residue fingerprint from its parents, and the gap is the thing that encodes it.

## What changed in Parts 1 and 2

- Part 1's Gap 2 example is wrong. Gap 2 is shielded against 3. A correction note now sits at the top of the post.
- Part 1's "R² ≈ 1.0" was not supported by the old model. The corrected model earns it.
- Both posts said "10 billion primes" and "100 billion primes." The tool scans integers up to <Latex formula="10^{10}" />, which contains about 455 million primes.
- Part 2 described a <Latex formula="10^{11}" /> run. The figures in it are from the <Latex formula="10^{10}" /> data, and the post now says so.
- Part 1 said the data "proves" the theory. It is a numerical check of a heuristic that remains unproven for any system of two or more linear forms.

The updated analyzer, with the three-case model, the size-matched baseline, a predicted-versus-observed chart and the regenerated <Latex formula="10^{10}" /> report, is at [github.com/johnmschoonover/prime_shield_analyzer](https://github.com/johnmschoonover/prime_shield_analyzer).

## Why a security engineer spent a year on this

The work I do for a living is detection engineering, and this project is the same job with cleaner data. A detection is a claim that some pattern is rarer in benign traffic than in malicious traffic. The claim is only worth anything if you measure the base rate, check the model against the full distribution instead of the examples that made you excited, and go back and say so when the first version was wrong. Gap 2 was my false positive. The <Latex formula="y = x" /> line is what a calibrated detector looks like. The habit of asking "is this already a known technique" before shipping is the one I most wish I had applied in Part 1.
