## The bill nobody itemizes

Working thesis: the license or ingest fee is the line item everyone sees. It's rarely the whole bill.

{/* INTERVIEW:
  - When someone asks you what a log source costs, which line items do you count? Ingest or license, compute, storage tiers, network, engineering time to onboard and maintain parsers, analyst time spent on the alerts it generates?
  - Which costs do people forget most often?
  - What does a parse failure cost? A log you pay to ingest but can't search is pure cost. (The published patent application on data-feed anomaly alerting fits here.)
*/}

## What a log is worth

Working thesis: a log's value is what it lets you detect, investigate, or prove. You can measure most of that.

{/* INTERVIEW:
  - How do you measure a log source's value? Rules that reference it, ATT&CK technique coverage, how often investigations use it, compliance requirements?
  - Do you have a rule of thumb, like the share of ingest that no detection rule ever touches?
  - How do you put a value on "we might need it in an investigation someday"?
*/}

## Four kinds of logs

Working thesis: once you have rough cost and value numbers, every source lands in one of four quadrants, and each quadrant has a default move.

{/* INTERVIEW:
  - Does this match how you think about it?
    - High value, low cost: keep it hot and searchable.
    - High value, high cost: optimize it with field trimming, filtering, or better parsing.
    - Low value, low cost: keep it cheap, in cold storage or a data lake.
    - Low value, high cost: drop it, or reroute it.
  - Give a real example for each quadrant, anonymized.
*/}

## The levers

Working thesis: "keep or drop" is a false choice. Teams have at least five levers between the two.

{/* INTERVIEW:
  - Rank these by how much they've saved you: filtering at the source, trimming fields, sampling, routing to cheaper tiers, aggregating or summarizing, shorter retention.
  - Where's your line on sampling and dropping? What would you never sample?
  - Hot SIEM versus a security data lake: how do you decide which data goes where?
*/}

## The rare-event trap

Working thesis: volume controls are dangerous when they treat every event alike, because the event that matters is often the rare one.
